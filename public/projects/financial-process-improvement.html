Financial Process ImprovementA Lean Six Sigma Case Study

A theoretical case study exploring how a household financial process can be measured, investigated and redesigned using Lean Six Sigma.

Presented by Glenn Charifa
01 / Baseline

The first signal is variance, not income

The case showed a recurring gap between what the household planned to spend and what it actually spent. The existing process lacked a dependable way to detect and control drift, causing finances to spiral out of control.

What the baseline analysis actually established

The Measure phase established that the problem was not simply “spending too much.” The process was producing recurring variance, was exposed to unexpected losses, and lacked controls that could identify drift early enough.

Baseline yield

The first measurable signal was process instability: staying within budget was not a dependable outcome.

02 / Investigation

Ask why, then ask it why, again

The case linked overspending to lifestyle mismatch, unmonitored credit cards and discretionary spending. Controls were designed to change the conditions around the behavior because telling people to spend less is unrealistic.

Root of the problemBudget adherence was weakened by limited monitoring, control mechanisms and structured financial planning.
03 / Intervention

Build the system that controls the budget

The proposed response was not a single tool. It was a set of controls designed around the causes identified in the analysis.

04 / Modeled outcome

The model moved in the right direction

The case exercise reports improved budget adherence after the proposed changes. This is a modeled certification outcome, not independently observed household performance.

Good automation starts with understanding.

Data does not exist in isolation. It is produced by people, processes, systems, assumptions and decisions. Before changing how data is reported or automated, understand what it represents, how the process works and where it can fail.

01

Understand before optimizing.

Map the process and establish the baseline before changing it.

02

Measure what matters.

Use measures that actually represent the behavior of the system.

03

Challenge the obvious answer.

Use different analytical lenses to test the same assumption.

04

Use technology as an enabler.

Automation and dashboards should make the right action more visible or more repeatable—not simply make the process look sophisticated.
